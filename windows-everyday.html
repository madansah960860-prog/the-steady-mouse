Windows Everyday

By the The Steady Mouse Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

A handful of small Windows skills make the whole computer feel friendlier: capturing the screen, finding what you saved, making things bigger, and the calm difference between a restart and a shutdown.

Taking a screenshot

A screenshot is a picture of whatever is on your screen. It is wonderfully useful for saving a confirmation, or showing a relative exactly what you are seeing.

  1. Hold the Windows key and press Shift and S together.
  2. The screen dims; drag a box around the part you want to capture.
  3. The picture is copied, ready to paste into an email with Ctrl and V.
  4. To keep it as a file, open the little preview that appears and click Save.

Tip: To capture the whole screen at once, press the PrtScn (Print Screen) key near the top-right of the keyboard, then paste it where you need it.

Getting comfortable with the mouse and keyboard

Before the particular skills, a word about the two tools you use for everything: the mouse (or the flat touchpad on a laptop) and the keyboard. A little ease with them makes every other task gentler, and ease comes only from unhurried practice.

The mouse has two buttons. The left one does almost everything — a single click to choose, a double click to open. The right one opens a little menu of extra choices; useful, but nothing to worry about at first. The small wheel between them rolls the page up and down. If the pointer ever feels as though it races away from you, that is not your hand — it is a setting, and you can slow it right down under Settings, Bluetooth & devices, Mouse.

On the keyboard, a handful of keys earn their keep. Enter confirms a choice or starts a new line. The long space bar adds a space. Backspace rubs out the letter to the left, like an eraser. And holding Shift while you press a letter makes it a capital. You do not need to learn the rest today; these few will carry you a long way, and the only path to comfort is doing them often enough that your hands stop thinking about it.

Finding your downloads

Anything you save from the internet — a photo, a form, a receipt — lands in the Downloads folder. Knowing where to look saves a lot of hunting.

  1. Click the yellow folder icon (File Explorer) on the taskbar.
  2. In the list on the left, click Downloads.
  3. Your most recent items are usually at the top.
  4. Double-click any file to open it.

Making everything bigger

You should never have to squint. Windows can enlarge menus and text across the whole computer.

  1. Open Settings, then Accessibility.
  2. Click Text size and drag the slider until it is comfortable, then click Apply.
  3. For a single web page, hold Ctrl and press the plus key to zoom in, or the minus key to zoom back out.

Tip: None of these settings touch your files — they only change how large things look. Adjust them as often as you like.

Keeping favorites on the taskbar

The taskbar along the bottom can hold your most-used programs so they are always one click away.

  1. Open a program you use often, such as your browser.
  2. Right-click its icon on the taskbar.
  3. Choose Pin to taskbar.
  4. It now stays there even after you close it; to remove it later, right-click and choose Unpin.

Adjusting the sound

Whether on a video call or watching a clip, the volume is controlled from one spot.

  1. Click the small loudspeaker at the bottom-right of the taskbar.
  2. Drag the slider right for louder, left for quieter.
  3. If you hear nothing, check the slider is not at the far left and the speaker has no line through it.
  4. Plugging in headphones switches the sound to them automatically, and unplugging them sends it back to the computer's own speakers.

Keeping the desktop tidy

A clear desktop is calmer to work on. A few habits keep it from filling with clutter.

  • Save files into the right folder — letters in Documents, photos in Pictures — rather than onto the desktop.
  • Give files names you will recognize later, such as “Insurance renewal 2026.”
  • Close programs you have finished with by clicking the × so the screen stays uncluttered.

Restart versus shut down

These sound alike but do different jobs, and knowing the difference is quietly powerful.

  • Restart turns the computer off and straight back on. It clears up a great many small glitches and is the first thing to try when something misbehaves.
  • Shut down turns the computer fully off until you press the power button again. Use it when you are finished for the day.
  1. Open the Start menu and click the power icon.
  2. Choose Restart to refresh, or Shut down to switch off.
  3. If anything is unsaved, save it first — the computer will remind you.

Making the whole computer easier to see and hear

There is no virtue in struggling with a screen that is too small or sound that is too quiet. Windows is full of generous adjustments, and using them is a sign of good sense, not of getting older.

For the eyes, the text-size slider under Settings and Accessibility enlarges menus across the whole computer, while holding Ctrl and pressing the plus key zooms in on any single web page. If the pointer is hard to follow, the same Accessibility settings let you make it larger and give it a bolder outline, so it never disappears against a busy page. There is even a Magnifier that enlarges whatever part of the screen you point at, handy for small print.

For the ears, the loudspeaker at the bottom-right sets the overall volume, and most keyboards have small speaker pictures along the top row that do the same with a single press. If you find certain sounds sharp, headphones often make speech clearer and calls more comfortable than the computer's own speakers. Spend a quiet quarter of an hour trying these, and the computer becomes a far more pleasant companion — none of it changes your files, so you can experiment without the slightest worry.

Letting Windows update

From time to time Windows improves itself with updates, which also close the gaps that scammers try to use. Letting them install is one of the best things you can do for your safety.

  1. Open Settings and click Windows Update.
  2. Click Check for updates and let any install.
  3. If asked to restart, pick a moment when you are not mid-task and allow it.

Tip: Keep a small notebook by the computer and jot down any step you had to look up. Writing it in your own words fixes it in memory far better than reading it again, and before long you will barely need the notebook at all.

Building confidence, one habit at a time

None of these skills needs to be learned all at once, and trying to cram them in is the surest way to feel overwhelmed. The people who grow comfortable with a computer are not cleverer than anyone else — they have simply done the small things often enough that their hands remember. Confidence is a by-product of repetition, nothing more mysterious than that.

So pick one skill a week and let it become ordinary before adding the next. Take a screenshot of something and email it to a friend. Find yesterday's download. Make the text bigger and decide whether you prefer it. Restart the computer deliberately rather than waiting for a problem. Each of these, done a few times, moves from “a thing I have to look up” to “a thing I just do,” and with every one the machine feels a little more like yours.

Keep this page bookmarked and return to it whenever a step slips your mind — there is no prize for memorising it, and no shame in checking. Every confident computer user you know relied on notes and second looks when they began; the only difference between them and you is a little more practice, and that comes with time. With these everyday skills growing steady, protect them all by learning the tricks of the trade in Scam Awareness.

Safety reminder

No honest company phones out of the blue to ask for payment, passwords, or access to your computer. If a call or pop-up does, it is a scam — hang up or close it.

Report scams at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
